One Question

One small non-academic question. One honest conversation.

Pocket Recall · Hold this in mind for the day
“You get one question to ask me today — anything that is not about school or chores.”

When to try

Natural transition moments: in the car, preparing dinner, or before lights out.

The parent’s role

Answer honestly and openly. No moralizing, teaching lessons, or turning it into a lecture.

The child’s role

Complete freedom to ask whatever comes to mind—funny, curious, or unexpected.

The problem we are looking at

Most daily conversations between parents and children naturally revolve around logistics: homework, tidying up, getting ready, and schedules.

Children rarely get to see their parents as real human beings with histories, memories, mistakes, and childhood quirks.

One Question inverts the everyday dynamic: your child asks, and you simply answer truthfully. It invites honest curiosity without academic pressure.

Prompts they might ask about

“What were you like when you were my age?”

“What was a funny mistake you made as a child?”

“What game or sport did you love playing most?”

“What was something you were scared of as a kid?”

✕ What NOT to do (Crucial Guardrails)

  • ✕
    Never turn your answer into a lecture or moral sermon. If they ask, “Did you ever hate homework?”, answer honestly. Do not tack on: “…but I always finished it because discipline is important.” Let the honest story stand on its own.
  • ✕
    Never dismiss silly or playful questions. If they ask, “Would you rather fly or turn invisible?”, engage playfully. Connection happens through lightness as much as seriousness.
  • ✕
    Never interrogate them immediately in return. Give your answer breathing room. Don’t immediately counter with: “Now what about you?” Let them process your answer.
  • ✕
    Never force the question on an exhausted day. If your child doesn’t have a question today, say: “No worries, you can ask tomorrow!”

The Golden Principle: Vulnerability Builds Trust

Children find immense comfort in knowing their parents weren’t always all-knowing adults. Hearing about your real childhood makes them feel understood in theirs.

Parent Field Notes

What to notice this week

After trying this over a few days, take note of what unfolds:

  • 01 Did your child ask spontaneously, or did you need to offer an invitation?
  • 02 Did they ask follow-up questions or laugh at something you shared?
  • 03 How tempting was it to steer the answer toward teaching a lesson?
  • 04 Did your child share something about their own life afterwards?
